Landslides have killed people in Alaska. NSF awarded UAF $1.77M to plan a digital slope copy.
A digital twin is only as good as the slopes it watches. The award abstract names none.

Per the NSF award record, the work runs from August 1st to July 31st, 2032 and is led by Carl Tape. It would pull ground shaking, satellite pictures, weather and groundwater into fast stand-in models.

The award abstract describes failures that stack. Heavy rain can soak a slope. An earthquake can weaken it. A later storm can bring it down.

Haines got at least eight inches of rain in two days in December 2020 per KHNS. Slides that week killed two people. A November 2023 slide in Wrangell killed six.

The limits are real. The award record is the only source found so far.

The University of Washington holds a larger partner award at $2.2M to UAF's $1.77M. Testing is planned in Alaska and the Pacific Northwest and no results exist.

At Barry Arm, state geologists track a slope of roughly 500 million cubic meters. If it failed fast, it could make a tsunami with life-threatening waves.

Which slopes get instruments first would decide what the twin is worth to Alaska.
