A borough in Alaska just wrote a weather rule. It's a form.
On October twentieth, the Kenai Peninsula Borough Assembly passed it, in effect right away.
Anyone modifying weather on borough land now needs a commercial lease or land use permit.
On land set aside for berry picking and skiing, geoengineering is banned outright.
The form asks for the chemicals, the flares, how often and how long the flights run, and the outcome they intend.
It followed August twenty third, when a California company, Rainmaker, flew a cloud seeding drone from two sites about twenty miles northeast of Homer.
By its own report, it dispersed about three hundred seventy five grams of silver iodide. Rainmaker says that made about a hundredth of an inch of rain, which it puts at nineteen million gallons across a hundred square miles.
It describes its forecasting software, Stratus, as picking the seeding windows from real time weather data.
So what can a borough reach? The borough mayor, Peter Micciche, says its power is "limited to borough lands at this point."
He also said, "We know that the FAA controls the skies."
And Alaska's Department of Natural Resources says state law doesn't address cloud seeding.
That limit isn't a failure. A land permit was never going to measure the weather.
The mayor says the borough was misled about what Rainmaker planned. Rainmaker's CEO says it had every permission it needed, and could have done more outreach.
The assembly has asked the Alaska Legislature to ban weather modification, or at least regulate it. Homer's Representative Sarah Vance says she'll sponsor a bill if she's re-elected.
