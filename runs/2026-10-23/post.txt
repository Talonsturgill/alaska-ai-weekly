A borough now makes cloud seeders file what a flight intends. A form can't say whether the rain was real.

On October 20th the Kenai Peninsula Borough Assembly passed a rule for weather modification on borough land. Anyone doing it needs a commercial lease or land use permit. The application asks for the chemicals, the flares, how often and how long the flights run, and the outcome they intend. Borough land set aside for casual use, like berry picking and skiing, is closed to geoengineering outright.

It followed a test on August 23rd. A California company, Rainmaker, flew a cloud seeding drone from two sites about 20 miles northeast of Homer. By its own report it dispersed about 375 grams of silver iodide. Rainmaker says that made about a hundredth of an inch of rain, which it puts at 19 million gallons across 100 square miles. It describes its forecasting software, Stratus, as picking the seeding windows from real time weather data.

The borough's reach stops at its own ground. Mayor Peter Micciche says its power is "limited to borough lands at this point" and notes that "the FAA controls the skies". Alaska's Department of Natural Resources says state law doesn't address cloud seeding. Every rain figure in the coverage is still the company's own. A fair first step, but measuring the answer takes the state or the federal government. Nothing in the coverage says either has started. The assembly has asked the Legislature to ban weather modification or at least regulate it. Representative Sarah Vance says she'll sponsor a bill if she's re-elected.

The form asks what a flight intends. Rainmaker says it checks results with radar and satellite data, and the news reports read for this post don't describe an outside check of that figure.

The mayor says the borough was misled about what Rainmaker planned. Rainmaker's CEO says it had every permission it needed and could have done more outreach.

If a borough can write down what a flight intends, who should write down what came out?
