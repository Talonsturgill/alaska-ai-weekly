LinkedIn caption

A drone out of Nenana flew 621 miles with human blood aboard. Then it landed where it took off.
The range is there now. The permission to use it isn't yet.

In January 1925 diphtheria threatened Nome. Antitoxin came by train to Nenana and 20 mushers ran it 674 miles by dog team. Nobody knew how a small engine would handle 40 below.

On August 19th pilots at UAF's drone center flew a Windracers ULTRA out of that same town. They flew it remotely. It stayed up 8 hours and 47 minutes and covered 1,000 kilometers in one loop. That is nearly the length of the serum trail.

It carried blood samples for the International Testing Agency, which runs anti-doping checks in sport. Each volunteer gave two. One flew and one stayed on the ground for comparison.

Cathy Cahill, who directs the center, told the Fairbanks chamber the flight proves the blood could survive, the News-Miner reported November 19th. No lab results have been published. Until they are, the fair claim is the trip and not the result.

The old cold question has an answer for small drones. UAF has flown them at 40 below.

The next gate is permission. Federal rules regulate against flying beyond the operator's line of sight. UAF plans to fit both of its ULTRAs with detect and avoid radar, which Cahill calls a key step toward medical delivery. Ryan Air and Tanana Chiefs Conference have partnered with the center to develop or test that delivery.

The need is concrete. Alaska's transportation department says 82 percent of the state's communities sit off the road system. The federal Medicare and Medicaid agency says medicine delays of 5 to 14 days are common in rural Alaska winters.

Dog teams proved the route a century ago. This drone has shown the range. The rest waits on published lab results and a radar that is still only planned.

If you run a clinic or a flight service off the road system, what would that radar have to prove before you trusted it with patient samples?
