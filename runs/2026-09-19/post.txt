In Cordova, Alaska, a rack of servers runs inside a hydroelectric powerhouse.

Greensparc put it there in 2024 and the turbine water cools it. Cordova is now getting an AI project too. The two have nothing to do with each other. That turns out to be the interesting part.

In August the University of Alaska Fairbanks said a four-partner team won $725,000 from the Energy Department's Genesis Mission to build AURORA-AI. UAF's share is $325,000 per Alaska's News Source. The others are Colorado State, the National Laboratory of the Rockies and Cordova Electric itself. It is a digital twin of the grid built from the co-op's own records. Forecast demand. Spot abnormal conditions. Choose water over fuel. Phase One begins October 1st and runs nine months.

Here is why Cordova is the right place to try it. The grid is an island. Eighteen megawatts and 1,566 customers with no wire to anywhere else. About eighty percent of the power is hydro from two creeks and the backstop is a 10.8 megawatt diesel plant. Guess demand wrong and the co-op can't buy from a neighbor. It burns fuel. A forecasting error there has a price you can count in gallons.

So the test is narrow and it is fair. By next June is there a measured fuel number or a demonstration? Richard Wies leads the project and calls nine months a really short period of performance. He is right.

Those servers matter after all. They are not part of the project. They are a load on the same grid so whatever the model learns to predict it has to predict them too.

One more thing worth saying plainly. Cordova is the easy case. It has hydro and engineers and records a model can use. Alaska's power cost program covers 193 communities that are largely reliant on diesel where power can cost three to five times what it does in more urban areas per the Alaska Energy Authority. Those grids need a forecast most and have the least data.

I want this to work. I also think the word AI has to be earned here rather than assumed. The cheapest way to earn it is to name the method it beats and then beat it in gallons.

What would convince you that a grid forecast actually worked?
