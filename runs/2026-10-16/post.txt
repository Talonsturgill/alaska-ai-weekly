USGS puts the odds of another magnitude 3 or bigger near Lake Minchumina this week at about 40 in 100, and it published the math.

The agency posted the odds twelve hours after a magnitude 5.6 earthquake there. Any forecast Alaskans lean on, human or machine, should show its work the way this one does.

The quake hit just past 3 a.m. Alaska time on October 15th. By October 16th, 103 people had filed felt reports.

For the week ahead, magnitude 4 or bigger sits near 7 in 100. Magnitude 5 or bigger is under 1 in 100. Magnitude 6 or bigger is under 1 in 1,000.

The method is a published equation from Reasenberg and Jones. It starts from how past aftershock sequences behaved and then bends toward this one.

When the forecast was made, one magnitude 3 or bigger aftershock had been counted. For the week, it prints a 95 percent range of zero to five such aftershocks.

One quake can't grade a probability, because 40 in 100 isn't wrong either way. USGS says its own model may perform poorly when big aftershocks set off their own aftershocks.

Sometimes the big one comes second. In 2011, a magnitude 7.3 in Japan came two days before a 9.1.

So the forecast doesn't prove it's right. It proves it can be checked. Every forecast carries the time of its next update.

The week isn't over, so the check isn't finished.

Odds. A range. A stated limit. A next check. That is the bar.

Which of those four does the AI tool you use at work leave out?
