A seal can be as small as two pixels. NOAA now calls the software that finds it super intelligence.
The name moved. The science behind it didn't.

NOAA's Alaska Fisheries Science Center keeps a page on its research tools. Last updated November 6th, it now calls its seal and polar bear detectors super intelligence. A tool that ages fish from their ear stones gets the same word.

The capability is real, and it's Alaska's.
Survey planes shoot color and thermal photos together from 1,000 feet.
NOAA says a million images may hold 7,000 to 9,000 animals.
Finding them by hand once took a year after every season.
The ear stone tool reads one in 30 to 50 seconds.
That's more than ten times faster than a person at a microscope.

NOAA's own 2019 article on the seal work calls it artificial intelligence. A NOAA whale project updated the same day says it was "previously known as Geospatial Artificial Intelligence for Animals."

A September 29th executive order tells federal agencies to say super intelligence instead, on websites and in reports.
The same order defines super intelligence as the technology the law already calls artificial intelligence.

The administration's case is that these systems "increasingly represent not merely artificial intelligence, but a new era of Super Intelligence." An associate dean for procurement law at George Washington University told GovCIO Media she knows of no purpose other than rebranding.

The page gives no accuracy figure for any of these tools.
A proposed federal definition is due to the President by November 28th.
Until a definition becomes law, the new word adds no capability the law recognizes.

If that definition sets a real capability bar, should Alaska's seal and fish tools have to clear it before they carry the word?
