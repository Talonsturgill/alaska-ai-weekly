A $10 million starting bid covers an AI app, its models and its users' chat content.
The chats are inventory now. Alaska helped put a privacy reviewer in front of the court first.

Character.AI filed for bankruptcy in October. State attorneys general say the app collected private chats, voice calls and mental health disclosures and used them to train its AI. The company plans to auction its assets on December 1st.

Alaska joined a bipartisan coalition of 40 attorneys general led by New York.

Alaska's Department of Law says the court ordered a consumer privacy ombudsman at the coalition's urging. The U.S. Trustee named Lucy Thomson to review the planned sale and recommend safeguards.

That's a real result for a state that joined rather than led. The ombudsman advises and the court decides. Any sale still needs approval after a hearing.

Character.AI has a case. It told the court its privacy policy already allowed the sale. The law itself asks what users could gain as well as what they could lose.

Then there's the buyer. The $10 million bidder was formed the day before the filing that disclosed its bid. Its backer isn't public. Disney bid $20 million for a technology license and not for the chats.

When a chatbot company fails, conversations people treated as private become an asset on a list. Any sale order should bind the next owner to the privacy terms users signed up under. A price alone shouldn't carry the chats out the door.

If you were advising the court, which one safeguard would you attach to any sale of these chats?
