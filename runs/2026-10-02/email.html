LinkedIn caption

A rockfish caught in the Aleutians in 2022 was born in 1878, by NOAA's estimate. Who counted its rings?

NOAA's Alaska Fisheries Science Center ages fish by their ear stones, called otoliths. Each one grows a ring every year like a tree. The Center's readers count the rings on more than 30,000 of them a year under microscopes. Those ages help managers set catch limits.

Now there's a faster reader. Near-infrared light hits a pollock otolith and produces a spectral signature. Machine learning ties that signature to an age. NOAA puts the efficiency gain at 600 to 800 percent. The microscope is still part of the process.

A model can only learn to read an age from stones whose ages have already been read.

NOAA's archive holds 2.5 million otolith pairs going back to the 1960s. Around 2 million of them once sat in a 1930s wooden airplane hangar in Seattle. NOAA says scientists trained these models on samples from that archive.

So the speed is real. It's also the payout on decades of patient human work that nobody called AI at the time.

In 2023 NOAA called getting machine-read ages into stock assessments the next step. Managers still set the limits. Machines don't.

For Alaska, the scarce input to useful AI is often a careful record somebody kept for decades. You can buy a spectrometer. You can't buy an archive that goes back to the 1960s.

Which Alaska record kept by hand deserves a model next, and who kept it?
