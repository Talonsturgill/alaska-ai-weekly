FERC hasn't ruled. The real fight is over who gets to shape the study.

DeepGreen, a Needham, Massachusetts company, asked FERC for a preliminary permit to study an underwater data center in Cook Inlet. The plan calls for about 100 megawatts, mostly for the data center.

A proposed 3.5 mile cable would land at Nikiski.

A preliminary permit only lets the holder study the site and claim first place in line to apply for a license. In the words of DeepGreen's Louis Wolfson, "a preliminary permit doesn't give us permission to build anything."

The deadline for objections was November 2nd. Kenai Peninsula Borough Mayor Peter Micciche called the approach sloppy. He said he hadn't had an email, letter or call from DeepGreen. The company said it was too early to reach out before the engineering and biology work is done on paper.

The Alaska Commercial Fisheries Conservation Alliance says the proposal didn't assess effects on more than 1,300 active permits. The Center for Biological Diversity filed to intervene.

DeepGreen has a fair point too. A study is how anyone gets answers, and this permit wouldn't allow construction. The order is the problem. Its neighbors got a federal objection deadline before they got a phone call.

Cook Inlet belugas have been listed as endangered since 2008. NOAA's best estimate is 331 whales, down from about 1,300 in 1979. A population that small needs its scientists in the room when the study is designed.

Before FERC rules, what should the borough, the fleet and NOAA's beluga scientists each need to see in the study terms?
