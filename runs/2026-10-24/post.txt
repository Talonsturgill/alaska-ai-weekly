Alaska makes about half of America's wildfire emissions on around 4 percent of federal firefighting money, per MIT Technology Review.

A fire satellite caught two Alaska wildfires in one image. Deciding which to fight is still a human rule.

Seeing is getting easier. A prototype satellite called FireSat caught the Moran and Chicken fires in one image in June 2025. Three operational FireSats reached orbit this July. Earth Fire Alliance aims for a scan every 20 minutes by the early 2030s.

The sensors are arriving faster than the rule for using them. On the Yukon Flats, a pilot program on 1.6 million acres sends crews only if they expect to contain a fire within 72 hours.

The Environmental Defense Fund says it intends to build an AI tool to help pick which fires to fight. That tool is still only planned.

The stakes sit underground. Near Fairbanks, a thaw probe hit frozen ground about a foot down on unburned soil. Inside a July 2021 burn scar it plunged at least three times deeper.

One 2007 fire released more than 2 million tons of carbon. The loss is the ground, not only the acres.

A Northern Arizona University scientist says it isn't clear the evidence shows suppression can have any impact on these fires. If that doubt holds, a faster eye can't save the ground. If it doesn't, letting fires burn costs the ground. Either way the argument worth having is over the rule, not the satellite.

That leaves three roads. Fight it, leave it, or light a mild fire on purpose. The 72 hour rule should be written in public and tested against the thaw probe evidence before any AI tool is asked to apply it.

Edward Alexander of the Gwich'in Council International puts the third road this way, "We need mild fire, not wildfire." A Council of Athabascan Tribal Governments crew plans cultural burns near Fort Yukon next spring. Those burns are still a plan.

When satellites can see every fire in the Interior, who should decide which ones get fought?
