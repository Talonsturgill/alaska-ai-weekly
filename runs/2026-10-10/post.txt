Within days of going up, a new plate reader camera at an Anchorage Lowe's was cut down.

The loudest argument is about the camera. The more useful one is about who writes its rules.

Flock's own FAQ says its cameras apply machine learning to read the plate, plus the make, color and body type of the vehicle. Neither Alaska's News Source nor the Anchorage Daily News calls the cameras AI. That's the vendor describing its own product.

Flock cameras went up on poles at multiple Anchorage Lowe's stores. Flock first told KTUU it was not aware of any cameras in Alaska. It then said it had begun installing them for a private customer. Flock hasn't named that customer.

Two proposed rules are expected to reach the Assembly for a vote October 20th, one from the mayor and one from Assembly members. Both would bind city agencies. The members' version would delete city camera data after 14 days. The mayor has said the members' version could "unduly limit some core functions of APD operations."

Neither proposal puts a duty on a store's camera. That leaves the pole answering to a retailer and a vendor.

Lowe's privacy statement says it typically keeps this kind of data no more than 90 days. Flock's FAQ sets a default of 7 days. Two separate documents, and neither is a city law.

The other side has real points. A store can put a camera on its own pole, and a mayor can worry about police operations. Neither answers the neighbors who want no camera at all. One resident told KTUU it's "very 'Minority Report.'"

Three questions travel well to any automated camera. What does it keep? For how long? Who gets it?

Which of the three should the Assembly settle first?
