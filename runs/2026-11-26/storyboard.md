# Ahead of the Patient (2026-11-26), board as locked at Gate 0

Alaska hospitals in the volunteer Alaska Stroke Coalition use RapidAI to read stroke scans. It flags a suspected large vessel occlusion, and in one case it alerted the doctor seconds after the scan, with the results reaching the local team and the clot specialists at once, in a state with five doctors who can pull out those clots. Before, just sending the images to Anchorage could take 45 minutes. In another case a man in his sixties came into an interior hospital after a fall; his team called Providence in Anchorage under 30 minutes after he arrived, two minutes before the official scan report, and the clot was out 4 hours 23 minutes after he arrived. Early, unpublished data shows about a 30 percent faster start on clot removal once patients reach Anchorage. The test: Providence's Kelly Isaacs says she can't really show statistically significant proof, Alaska has no consistent stroke outcome reporting, the software throws false positives; the clinicians' answer is that a doctor reviews every output and decides the transfer, and the six hospitals that got it free say they'll keep and pay. Federal rural health money, over $3.1 million, is set to build an AI imaging network across a planned 21 hospitals. Drawn as Ping, a round orchid alert pulse with a face born in a CT ring, that splits so the news reaches two places at once; in the second case a slow faceless report printer loses to a phone call; the film ends on a scoreboard whose outcomes row is a dashed blank.
