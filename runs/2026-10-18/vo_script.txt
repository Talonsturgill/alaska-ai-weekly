Press a blob against a boot, and it remembers the boot.
Meet Norm, a blob standing in for a machine that learns normal. Press the real boot back, and anywhere it doesn't fit, he glows red.
Three University of Alaska projects, shown at a statewide A I symposium, each start from one particular thing.
First, a ski turn. Jan Ronner, a former U A A alpine racer, is building FlowLine, an A I app that grades a skier's uploaded video and suggests drills.
Online advice, Ronner said, is very generic.
So picture Norm pressed with one skier's turn. Ronner plans to launch in December.
Second, a kitchen. Nathan Healy presented ANCHOR, built to help older Alaskans stay independent.
Motion sensors and one button learn a senior's routines, and it's designed to alert family when a routine slips past a safety threshold.
Higher stakes each time.
Third, a machine. Graduate student Aaron Smith uses digital twins, virtual copies of real equipment, to model compressors for a proposed gas pipeline.
Smith calls the compressor that pipeline's heartbeat, and the Daily News reports an estimate of more than fifty four billion dollars for the pipeline.
Do they work yet? That Daily News story, the only report found, names no users, no accuracy figures and no pilots.
These are showcase projects, so early is fair. Proof would be a December start that holds, a first household for ANCHOR, and a twin checked against a real compressor.
So what would you press Norm against first?
