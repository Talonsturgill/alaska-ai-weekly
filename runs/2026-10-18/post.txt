Three UAA AI projects were shown at a symposium, and that story reports no users, no accuracy figures and no pilots for any of them. Early is fair, but a symposium demo isn't evidence. These three should face the same proof test any vendor would.

The Anchorage Daily News reported on October 8th. Jan Ronner is a former UAA alpine racer building FlowLine, an app that grades a skier's uploaded video and suggests drills. Ronner calls online advice very generic and plans to launch in December.

Nathan Healy presented ANCHOR, built to help older Alaskans stay independent. It uses motion sensors and one button to learn a senior's routines, and it is designed to alert family when a routine slips past a safety threshold.

Graduate student Aaron Smith uses digital twins (virtual copies of real equipment) to model compressors for a proposed gas pipeline. Smith calls the compressor that pipeline's heartbeat. The Daily News reports an estimate of more than 54 billion dollars for the pipeline.

The video draws the shared idea as Norm, a stand-in for a machine that learns normal. Only ANCHOR is described as alerting.

Proof would be a December start that holds, a first household for ANCHOR, and a twin checked against a real compressor. Which of the three would you want proven first, and what would count as proof to you?
