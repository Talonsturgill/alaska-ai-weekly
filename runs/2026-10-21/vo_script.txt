She was counting the dead when one of them breathed.
Mid-September near Point Lay, college student Violet Anniskett photographed walrus carcasses for a count with U S G S researcher Tony Fischbach.
Then she saw a stomach move, a baby walrus between two carcasses, alive, bear tracks nearby.
Her first move wasn't a rescue. It was a question.
Machines are learning to spot walrus.
U S G S says its computing center is using trail camera photos to teach deep neural networks to spot walruses remotely.
People mark what's in a picture, and the network guesses, compares, and adjusts.
U S G S is also developing satellite methods to count carcasses, for Marine Mammal Protection Act reporting. Methods, not results.
One fair worry. If counting goes remote, fewer people may stand on beaches like that one.
These networks learn from pictures people collect, but they don't make the choice she faced next.
Walrus are hunted there for subsistence. "It was very important for us to ask the community first," she said.
It felt hard. "We can't choose this on our own," she said. Once the community decided, they went ahead.
They walked the calf to camp, got him through an old broken door into a cabin, and stayed the night.
He made a sad little hoo hoo and kept going to the window. By day two he slept against her.
Townspeople boated him across the lagoon. The Alaska SeaLife Center brought a kennel, and she flew with them to Anchorage, then drove to Seward, more than eight hundred miles away.
The SeaLife Center says he's doing well. His name is Annautaq, after her late father.
If satellites do the counting someday, who should still be on the beach?
