# Trained on 2019 (Dispatch 2026-11-27)

Concept. A study in Earth's Future (November 24th) by Andreas Krause's team at the Technical University of Munich trained an XGBoost machine learning model on lightning and weather observations from one year, 2019, six everyday weather readings and where ground strikes landed on a half-degree grid. On today's climate most of Alaska got under one strike per square kilometer a year. By 2100 under high emissions Alaska could get 116 to 594 percent more, the world's land 76 to 140 percent. Lightning fires burned at least 93 percent of Alaska's wildfire acres every year for a decade, but the study didn't model fire. Krause was quite surprised the projections were so high. Run backward the model estimated about 12 percent more lightning per degree; earlier studies projected 8 to 17; run forward its own 12 could nearly triple to 30, and the release says earlier studies differ mostly in math. The twist: a month earlier Krause and Anja Rammig posted a preprint in which a simple average beat the machine learning lightning models it tested, and advised testing models outside their training conditions. In fairness the paper's model beat a regression and three existing formulas. Read it as a range that waits on that test. Drawn as a teardrop glass instrument labelled THE MODEL with a wax seal stamped 2019, a hatched lemon range column beside a paper TODAY token, a dial whose two gear trains differ, a sleepy ruler that wins a race the model isn't in, and an envelope addressed ML LIGHTNING MODELS whose card ends on a stake beside the still-sealed glass.

Angle. A machine learning model that learned from 2019's lightning says Alaska could get more than double, up to nearly seven times, today's ground strikes under high emissions, and the honest read is a range: even its floor matters in a state where lightning fires burn nearly all the acres, while its ceiling is the question its own authors taught us to ask, will it hold outside the conditions it trained on.
