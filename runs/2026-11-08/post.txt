Two 1 km cables. 100 meters of data. No accuracy number in the public summaries.
The model is not the hard part. The record is.

Researchers buried two fiber optic cables under a road embankment in Utqiagvik.
Each is 1 km long.

A team led by Penn State built a digital twin from that data.
UAF's Dmitry Nicolsky is a co-author.
The data came from 100 meters of that road, recorded from September 2021 to June 2024.

The twin puts a neural network inside a heat transfer physics solver.
The network estimates unfrozen water content and thermal conductivity.
Eos editors call those soil properties uncertain.
Penn State's Ming Xiao writes that as new data arrives, the twin adjusts.
It was checked against separate seismic data, borehole temperatures and lab measurements.

Now the test.
It was tested on one road in one town.
It predicts ground temperature and soil properties, not when a road will fail.
The sources report no deployment.
The public summaries report no accuracy number.

The risk curve comes from a separate study of the Arctic Coastal Plain, not this twin.
Under high emissions it projects less than 10% of infrastructure at risk by mid century.
By the 2090s it projects about 80% of buildings, 60% of roads and 90% of pipelines.

Xiao says, "If we have, say, 10 more years of data, that would be extremely valuable."
That decade of record is the real build.

Which Alaska road, runway or pipeline should get the next fiber line?
Who should pay for the decade of data?
