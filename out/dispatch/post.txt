A ceiling in space weather may have been hiding in the measurement.

A new Nature study led by physicist Nithin Sivadas, with UAF coauthor Doğacan Öztürk, revisited how solar wind observations are matched with polar cap responses. The team used one-minute data from 1995 through 2019 and modeled uncertainty in arrival time, strength, location and evolution.

Those mismatches alone could draw a curve that looked like the response had saturated. After the uncertainty was calibrated, the average relation stayed linear through the observed limit of 15 millivolts per meter.

That changes the argument without erasing the boundary. Evidence stops at 15. Near 25 the projected geomagnetic response is roughly twice the older saturated estimate. That figure is extrapolated rather than observed. It does not mean doubled damage. Physical saturation could still appear beyond the data.

The larger lesson is about models. A more flexible fit can follow a biased curve with impressive precision while recovering none of the information the measurement blurred. No operational AI forecast was tested in this study.

Space weather can affect grids, GPS, aircraft and satellites. This result is not a forecast or current alert and it does not use an Alaska data set.

Where else are we treating a measurement boundary as a boundary in the world?
