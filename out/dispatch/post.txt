A mountain of rock near McCarthy sped up twice each year. Satellites caught both appointments.

The strange part is how much the map can prove before the story reaches underground.

Researchers used Sentinel-1 radar to map seven active rock glaciers near McCarthy. Parts of Sourdough Peak moved more than 160 centimeters per year. Photogrammetry checked more than 16,000 Sourdough Peak pixels. The two maps correlated at r 0.842 with mean absolute error of 10.72 centimeters per year.

Then researchers used unsupervised signal separation. They fixed the analysis at two components and interpreted one as long term motion and one as a seasonal pulse. That pulse appeared twice each year in association with snowmelt and later rain.

The surface measurement is the victory. The mechanism is the open question.

The authors modeled a path from sunlight to meltwater and a buried sliding layer. No borehole temperatures were recorded. The weather station sat six kilometers away. Two smaller glaciers complicated the simple sunlight pattern.

That is not a reason to distrust the map. It is a reason to put the next sensor underground.
