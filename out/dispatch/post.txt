A pollock model can change what an AI's apparent advantage means.

A paper published September 11th explores learned harvest rules for eastern Bering Sea pollock. Authors affiliated with the Alaska Fisheries Science Center helped build the research.

The agent practices harvest choices through simulated seasons. Feedback rewards outcomes and guides changes to its rule. Researchers then compare that learned policy with a predefined harvest rule.

The biological assumptions matter. In the constant-recruitment case, the learned policy leaves higher spawning biomass than the comparison. That's the weight of breeding fish. It also produces large annual catch swings.

A separate experiment links recruitment to the breeding stock. There the learned policy produces higher catches and lower spawning biomass than its own comparison. The first case's stock advantage doesn't carry across automatically.

That gives the research a useful job. It creates specific alternatives to examine against explicit goals. Both learned and predefined rules deserve that comparison.

NOAA Fisheries and the North Pacific Fishery Management Council manage Alaska pollock. Fishing businesses depend on sound management to plan their seasons. These illustrative simulations leave operational development ahead, with no demonstrated winning rule for the real fishery.
